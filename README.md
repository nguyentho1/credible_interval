# Bayesian Uncertainty in Heart Disease Prediction

## Introduction and Motivation

Clinical prediction models are commonly summarized using point estimates, confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values. However, these quantities do not always answer the probabilistic questions clinicians and researchers naturally ask.

For example, a 95% confidence interval should not be interpreted as having a 95% probability of containing the true parameter. Under the frequentist framework, the parameter is fixed, while the interval is generated by a procedure that would contain the true parameter in 95% of repeated samples.

Bayesian inference provides a different perspective. It combines prior information with observed data to produce a **posterior distribution**, allowing questions such as:

> What is the probability that this predictor is positively associated with heart disease?

or

> Given the observed data and model assumptions, what range contains 95% of the posterior probability for this effect?

This project explores this perspective using the UCI Heart Disease datasets, with particular attention to **credible intervals, posterior probabilities, prior sensitivity, predictive uncertainty, and model transportability**.

---

## Method

Four processed UCI Heart Disease datasets were combined:

* Cleveland: 303 observations
* Hungary: 294 observations
* Switzerland: 123 observations
* VA Long Beach: 200 observations

This resulted in **920 observations**. The original disease outcome (`num`) was transformed into a binary outcome, where `num = 0` represented absence of disease and `num > 0` represented presence of heart disease.

The analysis used Bayesian regression to estimate posterior distributions of predictor effects. Results were summarized using posterior odds ratios, 95% credible intervals, and posterior probabilities such as:

$$
P(OR>1\mid Data)
$$

The workflow also included:

* comparison of weak and informative priors using the Cleveland dataset;
* posterior patient-level risk estimation;
* train/validation/test evaluation; and
* leave-one-site-out validation to examine model transportability across the four data sources.

---

## Case Study

The analysis illustrates why posterior distributions can provide more information than a binary interpretation based on whether an interval crosses the null value.

For example, `oldpeak` showed a posterior median OR of approximately **1.72**, with a 95% credible interval of approximately **1.37–2.14**. Nearly all posterior probability was above OR = 1.

Age provided a more interesting example. Its posterior OR was approximately **1.21**, with a 95% credible interval of approximately **0.98–1.50**. Although the credible interval crossed 1, approximately **95.9% of the posterior distribution was above OR = 1**.

This demonstrates an important advantage of Bayesian inference: uncertainty does not need to be reduced to a simple “significant/non-significant” classification.

The Cleveland prior-sensitivity analysis provided another useful example. Estimates for male sex were very similar under weak and informative priors:

| Prior       | Posterior OR |   95% CrI | P(OR > 1) |
| ----------- | -----------: | --------: | --------: |
| Weak        |        ~3.52 | 1.43–8.74 |    ~99.7% |
| Informative |        ~3.46 | 1.42–8.54 |    ~99.7% |

In this example, the substantive conclusion was relatively robust to the choice of prior.

## Short Report

The conventional held-out test evaluation produced encouraging predictive performance, with ROC-AUC of approximately **0.903**, PR-AUC of **0.911**, sensitivity of **0.843**, and Brier score of **0.124**.

However, leave-one-site-out validation revealed substantially greater heterogeneity:

| External test site | ROC-AUC |
| ------------------ | ------: |
| Cleveland          |  ~0.832 |
| Hungary            |  ~0.879 |
| Switzerland        |  ~0.747 |
| VA Long Beach      |  ~0.701 |

This contrast is important. Strong performance after a conventional random split does not guarantee equivalent performance when a model is transferred to another clinical population.

The case study therefore highlights two different forms of uncertainty:

**parameter uncertainty**, represented through posterior distributions and credible intervals, and **transportability uncertainty**, revealed when the model is evaluated across different data sources.

Bayesian inference addresses the former elegantly, but it does not automatically solve dataset shift, measurement differences, selection bias, model misspecification, or poor external validity.

---

## Recommendations for Future Research

Future work should move beyond asking only whether a predictor has an effect and investigate whether the effect is **clinically meaningful**. Posterior probabilities could therefore be calculated against prespecified clinical thresholds, for example:

$$
P(OR>1.2\mid Data)
$$

rather than relying exclusively on \(P(OR>1)\).

Further research should also consider hierarchical Bayesian models that explicitly model between-site heterogeneity, more rigorous prior elicitation using external evidence, posterior predictive checks, calibration analysis, decision-curve analysis, and prospective external validation.

Most importantly, Bayesian clinical prediction should not be viewed simply as replacing a 95% confidence interval with a 95% credible interval. Its greater value lies in treating uncertainty as part of the scientific result itself:

> **Bayesian inference does not eliminate uncertainty—it allows uncertainty to become part of the answer.**
